# CSI: Naches — Teacher Guide (Grades 5–12)

## Purpose

Use narrative-driven forensic science to practice observation, data comparison, causal reasoning, mathematical modeling, evaluation of sources, and science argumentation. The game uses fictional local-region settings but **does not** portray real crimes, persons, companies, or school incidents.

The inquiry sequence broadly supports the *science and engineering practices* within Washington's NGSS-based Science Learning Standards: developing explanations, analyzing and interpreting data, using mathematical thinking, and obtaining/evaluating/communicating information. It is **not** a formally aligned grade-level assessment, and the grades 5–12 options currently adjust written-response expectations rather than fully adapting every passage or every science model.

## Case itinerary

| Case | Fictional scenario | Science emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| The Vanishing River Logger | A missing research sensor by the Naches River | Environmental measurements, humidity, travel time |
| The Canyon Sample Switch | Mixed labels on habitat specimens near Cowiche Canyon | Soil texture, evidence provenance, data comparison |
| The Orchard Signal | Misleading frost alert near Tieton | Sensor placement, temperature measurements, error diagnosis |
| The Greenway Exhibit Puzzle | A display panel appears replaced by the Yakima Greenway | Prints vs. pigments, exposure, material comparison |
| The Silent Signal | A fictional Selah-area research station loses power | Circuits, Ohm's law, timestamps and digital evidence |

**Suggested pacing:** 20–30 minutes per case depending on reading fluency and how much reasoning is required. Suggested 30-minute period: briefing 3 minutes, evidence/people 9–12 minutes, labs 7–9 minutes, timeline 2 minutes, written conclusion 5–8 minutes. This is a planning estimate, not a measured norm.

## Expectations

Students must record a hypothesis, correctly interpret all 7 evidence exhibits and 4 interviews, run **at least two distinct readings per model** in each of 3 laboratory tasks, solve a numerical problem, arrange 4 source events, and submit a final theory supported by 2 useful exhibits and a 3-part report. The game verifies its answer key and minimum writing length, **not** the scientific quality of students' explanations.

| Selected grade | Automated minimum per report section | Human expectation |
|---|---|---|
| 5–6 | 10 words | Name a clear observation and avoid overclaiming |
| 7–8 | 17 words | Connect multiple sources and show a causal chain |
| 9–10 | 23 words | Consider an alternate interpretation or limitation |
| 11–12 | 30 words | Evaluate reliability, error, and plausible alternatives |

These thresholds are writing scaffolds, not word-count-based grades.

## Suggested teacher rubric — 20 points

- **Question and hypothesis (4):** testable initial explanation and revision in light of evidence.
- **Evidence integrity (4):** distinguishes measurement and testimony; cites at least two appropriately relevant exhibits.
- **Science and mathematics (4):** analyzes multiple lab trials and correctly uses units and relationships.
- **Timeline and alternative explanations (4):** accurately organizes sequence, checks inconsistencies, and does not overstate certainty.
- **Claim–Evidence–Reasoning (4):** clear conclusion, cited records, scientific rationale, and meaningful limitation.

### Checks before classroom use

- Pilot the case yourself and verify every model description for the lesson goal you intend to teach.
- For high school, discuss limitations of simplified forensic models and weak evidence such as footprints or clothing description.
- Do not assess real-life guilt or make real accusations. The case answers identify who performed a fictional action, not necessarily a criminal act.
- Students can use the notebook's download icon to export a text report for the teacher.
- Avoid full names or private student information. Progress is stored only in that browser.

## Safety and scientific accuracy

The models are explicitly illustrative; e.g. a drying index, sensor lag index, and exposure score are educational proxies rather than validated diagnostic measurements. They are not suitable for field safety decisions, forensic identification, predictions, or legal conclusions. All cases avoid physical experiments with hazardous equipment or substances.
